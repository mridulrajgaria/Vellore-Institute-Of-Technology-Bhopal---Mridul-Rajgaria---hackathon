The engine converts unstructured financial text into consistent, explainable information that a portfolio strategy can consume.
Information overload
Too many headlines and posts to assess consistently by hand.
Tweets, NewsAPI and GDELT are processed into one schema with cross-label deduplication.
Black-box distrust
A score is less useful if its reasoning cannot be inspected.
A linear impact score (1.0 to 10.0) built from 4 named, audit-ready components, configurable via YAML.
Execution drag and noise
Reacting to every small change can cause excessive trading.
Rebalancer with ±0.20 deadband, EMA half-life 2.0 days and a 10% daily turnover cap.
Reproducibility
A reviewer needs to reproduce the result without live APIs.
Deterministic committed sample datasets (<5 MB): full audit and replay with zero API keys or live downloads.

5 • Impact scoring
Event severity 35% Regulatory = 0.9
Sentiment strength 30% |s| × c = 0.72
Source credibility 20% GDELT 0.85
Mention volume 15% 0.40 (illustrative)
Credibility: GDELT 0.85, NewsAPI 0.80, Kaggle Tweets 0.50. Severity: Regulatory/Credit 0.9, Earnings 0.7, Product 0.5, Other 0.3. Volume: log-scaled tweet density vs rolling median.
Impact-score formula
Impact = 1.0 + 9.0 × ∑(w_i × Component_i) ∈ [1.0, 10.0]
1.0 + 9.0 × (0.35×0.90 + 0.30×0.72 + 0.20×0.85 + 0.15×0.40) = 1.0 + 9.0 × 0.761 = 7.85
Combines event importance, sentiment, source reliability and mention activity into one consistent severity measure.
What the score means, and does not
It ranks how severe a piece of text looks. It is not a probability of a price move or a promise of trading return.
Every score on the dashboard can be unpacked via “Show the math” into its 4 raw component values.

How a risk score nudges portfolio weights, within limits
If all 14 stocks start with equal weights, each is 1 / 14 ≈ 7.14% of the portfolio. Signals tilt that baseline; rules keep the tilt bounded.
Equal-weight portfolio → Risk signal → Bounded adjustment
Conceptual example, not an application output. 14 hypothetical stocks with invented smoothed scores (A +0.40, B −0.40, C +0.20, D −0.20, ten at 0), run through the exponential tilt with k = 1.50. Here neither the weight bounds nor the 10% turnover cap binds (one-way turnover 7.65%); they would limit more extreme scores. Not an optimal-allocation claim.
1 • Daily score Per signal: |s| < 0.20 is ignored (deadband); negative s is multiplied by 1.25. Signals are averaged, weighted by attribution × impact / 10.
2 • EMA smoothing
half-life 2.0 days		 score = (1−α)·prev + α·raw, α = 1 − 2^(−1/2) ≈ 0.293. A brief burst of news cannot cause an immediate swing.
3 • Exponential tilt
k = 1.50		 w ∝ exp(k × score), normalised to sum to 1. Positive scores raise a stock above 7.14%; negative scores lower it.
4 • Weight bounds
3.57% to 14.29%		 Weights are projected into 0.5× to 2.0× of the 7.14% baseline, with the excess redistributed, so no stock gets too small or too large.
5 • Turnover cap
10% one-way / day		 If the move exceeds the cap, trades are scaled down; weights are then renormalised to exactly 1.0.
Costs, fallback
5 bps		 Backtest deducts 5 bps on turnover from net NAV. Missing market data reverts to equal weight, 7.14%.

Where the model is weak, and what could come next
MODEL LIMITATION • MEASURED RESULT
A model that performs well on curated financial headlines may perform differently on informal social posts and other real-world text. Informal language, ambiguity and writing style are plausible causes, but they are hypotheses, not tested here.
FinBERT is precise on bad news (0.85), useful for risk detection, but misses subtle positive analyst targets (positive recall 0.30). Accuracy = share of texts labelled correctly; macro-F1 averages per-class scores.
